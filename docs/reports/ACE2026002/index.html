A randomized controlled trial of progressive resistance training in depressed elders

Abstract
This report audits a randomized controlled trial by Singh, Clements, and Fiatarone (1997) that compared 10 weeks of progressive resistance training with an attention control condition in 32 community-dwelling adults aged 60 and over with major depression, minor depression, or dysthymia. Every table in the article labels the values reported in parentheses as standard deviations, yet the Methods state that they are standard errors. The article’s test statistic values support the Methods (i.e., the parenthetical values are standard errors). The reported age ranges are impossible under the reported standard deviations. Additionally, the depression subgroup means in Table 4 are impossible under the whole-group standard deviations in Table 3. The largest post-test standardized mean difference that the article can support on any depression outcome is −0.72. We located 24 meta-analyses that report a study-level effect size for this trial. Of these, 19 meta-analyses assign Singh (1997) a value larger than any possibly correct extraction of the effect size. The median assigned value is −2.74 and the largest is −5.04. Four report exactly −1.75, which is due to meta-analysts treating the parenthetical values of the BDI scores as standard deviations. One of these meta-analyses is a Cochrane review. Furthermore, five of the 10 categorical baseline p values can not be reproduced by any standard test of the reported contingency table cells. Later in the paper there is a reported subgroup mean that is impossible given its sample size. In total, this audit identifies 110 instances of Research Integrity Violations across two categories. Due to the pervasive contamination the misreporting has had on the evidence syntheses in this field, we recommend retraction.

1 Executive summary

1.1 Description of audited study

This report audits Singh (1997), a highly cited RCT of progressive resistance training as a treatment for depression in older adults. The trial has two arms. The exercise arm received 10 weeks of high-intensity resistance training, three sessions per week (n=17n = 17). The attention control arm attended an interactive health education program twice a week for 10 weeks (n=15n = 15). Subjects were community-dwelling volunteers aged 60 to 84 who met DSM-IV criteria for major depression, minor depression, or dysthymia. Depression was measured at baseline and at 10 weeks (post-intervention) with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD), the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), and a count of DSM-IV symptoms. The article also reports quality of life (SF-36), self-efficacy, morale, strength, and physical function. All 32 subjects completed the trial. The authors conclude that resistance training is an effective antidepressant in depressed elders.

The trial was one of the first RCTs of resistance training for depression. Meta-analyses of exercise and depression have included Singh (1997) for nearly three decades. The problems documented in this report concern how the summary statistics are labeled and reported. Those summary statistics are the values that meta-analyses extract.

1.2 Impact of audited study

We call a work that cites the audited study infected, because its findings incorporate results that this report identifies as unreliable. According to OpenAlex (retrieved 2026-09-20), 440 articles cite the study (infected articles). Of these, 77 are meta-analyses or reviews (infected reviews). The infected reviews have themselves been cited 12450 times, which measures how far the audited results have spread through the evidence base. The audited study or an infected review is cited by 4 clinical guidelines or consensus statements (see Table 1).

Note. Counts are from OpenAlex, retrieved 2026-09-20, and depend on its coverage; other databases (Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar) give different totals. Infected articles is the number of works indexed by OpenAlex that cite the paper. Infected reviews is the subset of those works that OpenAlex types as a review or whose title indicates a meta-analysis or review. Spread via reviews is the total number of citations received by the infected reviews in that row, as a measure of how far the paper’s results have propagated; a review that cites more than one audited paper is counted in each row, and a work citing several of those reviews is counted once per review, so rows and the total can overlap. Spread to guidelines is the number of distinct works whose title indicates a clinical guideline, consensus, or position statement and that cite the paper directly or cite one of its infected reviews; only guidelines published in journals are indexed, so policy documents from agencies such as WHO or NICE are not counted, and a guideline reached from more than one audited paper appears in each row.

The values in parentheses in the article’s tables are standard errors (SEs), although every table labels them as standard deviations (SDs), as Section 2 shows. An SD describes how widely the subjects’ scores are spread around their group mean, whereas an SE describes how precisely that mean has been estimated and equals the SD divided by the square root of the group size, so in groups of 15 and 17 subjects the SE is about a quarter of the SD. A meta-analyst extracts the mean, the SD, and the sample size of each group from a results table such as Table 3 of Singh (1997), whose footnote reads “Means and (SD)”, and computes a standardized mean difference (SMD), the difference between the two group means divided by their pooled SD, so that the effect is expressed in SD units. Taken at face value, the post-intervention BDI scores of 9.8 (2.4) and 13.8 (2.0) give an SMD of −1.75 (negative values favor exercise throughout). Read as SEs, the same scores give −0.44. The two readings differ by a factor of about four on every depression measure, because dividing by an SE in place of an SD shrinks the denominator by 17\sqrt{17} or 15\sqrt{15}, both close to four. Across the six depression outcomes and under Cohen’s d, Hedges’ g, or Glass’s delta, the three SMD formulas in common use, the largest post-test SMD that the article’s own numbers can support is −0.72. A post-test effect size beyond −0.72 did not come from a correct reading of the article.

We located 24 meta-analyses that include Singh (1997) and report a study-level effect size for the trial (see Table 2 and Figure 1). Most extracted post-test effects, which compare the two groups’ scores at 10 weeks. Three, Gordon (2018), Dai (2024), and Li (2026), extracted change-score SMDs, which compare how much each group’s scores changed between baseline and 10 weeks. A change-score SMD can be larger than the post-test SMD from the same trial, here because the exercise group started with higher depression scores and fell further. Gordon’s value of −1.03 is the difference between the groups in GDS change divided by the pooled baseline SD. Dai’s value of −1.28 is the difference in BDI change divided by the SD of the change scores, which depends on the correlation between each subject’s baseline and 10-week scores, at a correlation of 0.8. Both reproduce exactly when the values in parentheses are read as SEs. Li does not report the correlation it assumed, but its value of −1.62 is consistent with a change-score SMD under the correct reading at a high correlation. We count all three as defensible, together with the two post-test values within the bound (Lawlor 2001; Liu 2011). The other 19 meta-analyses extracted post-test effects and assign values beyond −0.72, which no correct post-test extraction can produce. A change-score extraction would not rescue most of them either. A change score standardized by the baseline SD can reach at most −1.72 on any outcome, whereas one standardized by the SD of the change can reach at most −1.89 at a correlation of 0.8. Of the 19, 15 are beyond both bounds. The other four report exactly −1.75, the value that the post-test misreading produces, as the next paragraph shows. The median effect assigned to this trial is −2.74 and the largest is −5.04 (Yu 2022).

The values that recur across meta-analyses show how the error was made. Four meta-analyses (Cooney 2013; Heinzel 2015; Rhyner 2016; Kvam 2016), one of them the Cochrane review, report exactly −1.75, the Hedges’ g that results when a meta-analyst treats the values in parentheses for the post-intervention BDI scores as SDs. The values of −2.70 and −2.77 (Krogh 2017; Silveira 2013) are Hedges’ g and Cohen’s d for the post-intervention HRSD scores under the same misreading. In these cases the arithmetic is correct and only the input is wrong, because the meta-analysts did what the table told them to do. The other values beyond the bound can not be reproduced from any single outcome in Table 3 under either reading, so their inputs can not be identified from the values alone. Four of them (Schuch 2016a, 2016b; Hooper 2018; Rossi 2024) report −3.11 or −3.10. The network meta-analysis of 218 trials by Noetel (2024) publishes its extracted data, in which the baseline and 10-week values of this trial appear with the values in parentheses entered as SDs (1.8, 2.4, 1.7, and 2.0 for the BDI). Its model is arm-based, with each arm’s change from baseline divided by that arm’s baseline SD and corrected for small samples, and gives the exercise arm a standardized change of −6.06 on the BDI against −2.55 for the control arm, a difference of −3.52 (−5.03 on the HRSD). All of these values are larger than any reading of the article’s tables can produce.

# A DOI becomes a link. One entry (a thesis) has only a ProQuest URL, which
# contains characters that break a LaTeX table cell, so the PDF shows a label.
doi_link <- function(doi) {
  is_doi <- !grepl("^http", doi)
  url <- ifelse(is_doi, paste0("https://doi.org/", doi), doi)
  if (knitr::is_html_output()) {
    sprintf('<a href="%s">%s</a>', url, ifelse(is_doi, doi, "ProQuest"))
  } else {
    ifelse(is_doi, sprintf("\\href{%s}{%s}", url, doi), "ProQuest (thesis)")
  }
}

# Each study label ("Cooney 2013") is also its citation key ("cooney2013"),
# so the cell can link to the entry in the reference list: an anchor in
# HTML, and \citeproc (what pandoc emits for a citation) in LaTeX, which
# hyperref turns into the same link as an in-text citation.
cite_link <- function(study) {
  key <- tolower(gsub("\\s+", "", study))
  text <- sub(" ", " (", study)
  text <- paste0(text, ")")
  if (knitr::is_html_output()) {
    sprintf('<a href="#ref-%s">%s</a>', key, text)
  } else {
    sprintf("\\citeproc{ref-%s}{%s}", key, text)
  }
}

1.3 Summary of findings

The values in parentheses throughout the article are standard errors, although every table labels them as standard deviations. The article’s own age ranges, subgroup means, and test statistics show this (Section 2). Read as labeled, the trial’s effect on depression is about four times larger than the data support. Of the 24 meta-analyses we located, 19 carry the inflated effect. Separately, the column labeled “Baseline p-value” in the outcome tables does not report baseline comparisons, 8 other reported p values can not be reproduced under any reading, two reported SDs are impossible given their ranges, and one subgroup mean is impossible given its sample size (Section 3). In total, this audit identifies 110 instances of Research Integrity Violations. We recommend retraction (Section 4).

2 The values in parentheses are standard errors

Findings in this report are labeled using version 1.0 of the ACE Research Integrity Violations taxonomy (ACE-RIV). A reported value that is wrong as labeled but consistent with the rest of the article once the statistic is read as what it actually is (here, a standard error labeled as a standard deviation) is recorded under RIV 2.4, Data Miscalculation or Erroneous Reporting. A reported value that no reading makes consistent with the article’s other numbers, such as a p value that no test of the reported data can return or an SD larger than its range allows, is recorded under RIV 2.2, Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets. Every finding in this section is recorded under RIV 2.4.

2.1 The labels and the Methods disagree

Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7 of Singh (1997) report the summary statistics for the baseline characteristics and outcomes. The footnote of each of these tables states that the values are “Means and (SD)”. Yet the statistical analysis section of the Methods states that they are standard errors (see Figure 4).

Continuous data are described as the mean (standard error) or median and range as appropriate, except for quality of life measures which are presented as mean (standard deviation) to allow comparison with reported normal values.

Both statements can not be true, but the article reports enough to determine which one is correct.

2.2 Values that can not be standard deviations

Beneath the mean age of each group, Table 1 reports the age range, 61 to 82 years in the exercise group and 60 to 84 years in the control group. A standard deviation limits how far apart the youngest and oldest subjects can be. The widest range a group can have for a given SD arises when one subject sits at each end of the range and every other subject sits exactly at the mean, because that arrangement spreads the ages as far as possible while adding the least to the SD. For a group of nn subjects with standard deviation ss, that widest possible range is s2(n−1)s\sqrt{2(n-1)}. A reported range wider than this can not come from a group with that SD. If the value of 1.5 for the exercise group is an SD, even one as large as 1.55 before rounding, the ages of its 17 subjects could span at most 8.8 years, against a reported span of 21 years. If the value of 2.0 for the control group is an SD, the ages of its 15 subjects could span at most 10.8 years, against a reported 24 years. If the value of 1.2 in the abstract is an SD, the ages of all 32 subjects could span at most 9.8 years, against a reported 24 years. The values in parentheses can not be SDs. If they are standard errors (SEs), the implied SDs are 6.2, 7.7, and 6.8 years, which fit the reported ranges.

The outcome tables can also be checked against each other, because Table 4 breaks the BDI and HRSD outcomes of Table 3 down by diagnostic subgroup, with 7 exercise and 10 control subjects with minor depression and 9 exercise and 4 control subjects with major depression (see panel B of Figure 5). The one dysthymic subject in each group is in neither subgroup. The variance of a whole group (the square of its SD) equals the variance within the subgroups plus the variance between the subgroup means. Neither part can be negative, so the spread of the subgroup means alone puts a lower bound on the SD of the whole group, whatever the spread within each subgroup. For the exercise group at baseline, the subgroup BDI means of 16.7 and 24.6 around a whole-group mean of 21.3 require a whole-group SD of at least 3.87. Table 3 reports 1.8. This bound holds with every reported mean free to fall anywhere within its rounding interval (the values that round to the reported one, 21.25 to 21.35 for a reported 21.3), in whichever direction lowers the bound, with the reported value taken at the top of its own interval, and with the dysthymic subject ignored. The same calculation for all 8 BDI and HRSD values in Table 3 gives lower bounds from 1.31 to 5.51, and every reported value, from 0.9 to 2.4, is below its bound. None of these values can be SDs. Read as SEs, the implied SDs, from 3.7 to 9.9, are all above their bounds.

2.3 Reported p values reproduce only as standard errors

Every reported p value in these tables can be recalculated from the reported means, the values in parentheses, and the group sizes. We recalculated each one twice, once reading the values in parentheses as standard deviations (SDs) and once as standard errors (SEs). Every reported value is rounded, so each mean and parenthetical value may sit anywhere in its rounding interval. Table 1 and Table 2 report unpaired comparisons, which we tested with Student’s and Welch’s t tests. Each outcome table reports a Group × Time p value for every variable, which tests whether the two groups changed by different amounts over the 10 weeks. In a repeated measures ANOVA with two groups and two time points, that p value is the t test comparing the change scores (each subject’s 10-week score minus their baseline score) of the two groups. The SD of a change score depends on the correlation between the baseline and 10-week scores, which the article does not report. We therefore let that correlation take any value from −1 to 1, separately in each group, which gives the widest range of p values each reading could produce. Allowing every correlation can only make the test more lenient. Under either reading the range runs from near zero, where the correlation is close to 1 and the change scores are tightly clustered, up to a ceiling set by a correlation of −1, where they are most spread out. A reading is ruled out only when the reported p value lies above that ceiling, so that no correlation could have produced it. Each range is rounded outward to the precision of the reported value. A reading is possible when the reported p value falls inside its range (see Table 3 and Table 4). Table 5 serves as a check on the method, because the Methods identify its parenthetical values as SDs. Read as SDs, all 8 of its Group × Time p values reproduce, whereas three of them do not reproduce with the values read as SEs.

For 25 variables in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7, no reading of the parenthetical values as SDs can return the reported p value, whereas every one of them reproduces with the values read as SEs. For medications per day in Table 1, the SD reading gives .00–.01 against a reported .36, and the SE reading gives .25–.46. For HRSD in Table 3, the SD reading gives .000–.001 against a reported .008. For lifting self-efficacy in Table 6, it gives .00–.55 against a reported .59. For the other variables the test does not separate the two readings, either because the reported p value is very small or very large or because the range under the SD reading is wide enough to contain it. ADL in Table 7 reproduces only as SDs, because the control group’s baseline parenthetical value of 0.1 is far smaller than the other three values in the row (0.8, 0.8, and 0.9). It is described but not counted.

3 Reported statistics that no reading can recover

The values in this section are wrong under either reading of the parentheses, so each is recorded under RIV 2.2.

3.1 Baseline p values that are not baseline comparisons

Tables 3 through 7 of Singh (1997) each contain three p value columns labeled “Baseline”, “Time”, and “Group × Time”. The Methods state that a repeated measures ANOVA was used “to analyze the effect of time, treatment, and time × treatment interactions”, which is three effects. A reader would take the first column to be a comparison of the groups at baseline. The Methods name three effects for three columns, so the first column most likely reports the treatment main effect of that ANOVA, the average difference between the groups across both time points, which is not a comparison of the groups at baseline. Table 5 is the place to test whether the column is a baseline comparison, because it is the only table where the Methods identify the values in parentheses as SDs (see Figure 6).

We recalculated an unpaired t test on the baseline scores of each of the 8 SF-36 subscales from the reported means, SDs, and sample sizes (see Table 5). With every reported mean and SD free to sit anywhere within its rounding interval, 7 of the 8 reported p values fall outside the range that either t test can return, each by more than 0.2. The exception is mental health, where the groups start and end close together. Reading the SDs as SEs does not reproduce any of the 8. For bodily pain, the baseline means of 56.8 and 72.3 with SDs of about 30 give .14, against a reported p value of .98. For social functioning, the baseline means of 51.5 and 69.2 give .06, against a reported .66. For role emotional, the baseline means of 43.1 and 60.0 give .23, against a reported .91.

The mislabel matters most where a baseline comparison matters. For bodily pain, social functioning, and role emotional, the groups start 15 to 18 points apart and end close together. A reader of Table 5 would take the reported values of .98, .66, and .91 to mean that the groups started out alike.

The same column appears in the depression outcomes in Table 3 (see Figure 7). We recalculated the baseline t test for each of its six measures, treating the values in parentheses as SEs (see Section 2 and Table 5). None of the six reported p values can be reproduced under either reading of the parentheticals. One of these discrepancies changes the article’s account of the trial. On the somatic subscale of the BDI, the exercise group started at 8.2 and the control group at 6.5, with SEs of 0.6 and 0.4. The baseline t test returns a p value of .029 (.026 with Welch’s test), against .98 in Table 3. The Results state that “there were no significant differences at baseline in any variables measured.” The somatic subscale is also the outcome with the strongest reported interaction (p<.0001p < .0001), on which the group with the higher baseline score improved the most. A group that starts with more symptoms has more room to improve, so a baseline difference on this subscale bears on the strongest result in the article.

The column is mislabeled in all five tables. The values in it can not be recovered as baseline comparisons under any reading of the parentheticals. Each one that no test of the baseline scores can return is an instance, 7 in Table 5 and 6 in Table 3, for 13 instances of Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets (RIV 2.2). Tables 4, 6, and 7 contain the same column and are described here but not counted. The article reports no baseline comparison for any outcome, although it states that none differed at baseline. For at least one outcome, the somatic subscale of the BDI, the article’s own numbers show a baseline difference.

3.2 p values that can not be reproduced under any reading

Three of the unpaired comparisons in Table 3 reproduce under neither reading of the parentheticals. For ethanol intake in Table 1, the reported p value is .34, against .02–.07 as SDs and .56–.63 as SEs. For the duration of depressive symptoms, it is .45, against .01–.06 and .50–.62. For hospital days in Table 2, it is .40, against .00–.05 and .41–.60. All three variables are skewed, with SEs close to the size of the means, which points to a few subjects with large values. Such variables may have been summarized and tested differently (the Methods allow for medians and ranges “as appropriate”). As reported, they are three instances of Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets (RIV 2.2).

Table 1 of Singh (1997) contains a p value for each of 10 categorical baseline characteristics (see Figure 8). The article states that p values for categorical data come from either a chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, without saying which variable uses which. The Results identify the test through three comparisons of proportions reported with their p values: response to treatment in 10/17 exercisers vs 4/15 controls (p=.067p = .067), remission of minor depression in 6/7 vs 4/10 (p=.06p = .06), and remission of major depression in 8/9 vs 2/4 (p=.12p = .12). Pearson’s chi-square test without a continuity correction reproduces all three (.067, .059, and .125, respectively). Fisher’s exact test reproduces none of them (.087, .134, and .203, respectively). The authors therefore used an uncorrected chi-square test, even for tables with expected cell counts well below five, where the chi-square approximation is unreliable and Fisher’s exact test is the usual choice.

We recalculated this test, and Fisher’s exact test, from the contingency table cells reported in Table 1 (see Table 6). Four of the 10 p values reproduce under the uncorrected chi-square test, those for annual income, previous treatment by a psychiatrist, previous antidepressant drugs, and previous hospitalization. Neither test reproduces the other six. For gender (5 men and 12 women vs 7 men and 8 women), the two tests return .31 and .47 against a reported p value of .71. For residence (13 house and 4 apartment vs 12 and 3), they return .81 and 1.00 against a reported .63. Two variants of the chi-square test, the Yates continuity correction and the likelihood ratio test, do not change this. None of the six 2 by 2 tables returns its reported value under the Yates corrected chi-square test. The likelihood ratio test agrees with the uncorrected chi-square test to the reported precision. Before counting a p value as irreproducible, we also tried every way of collapsing the categories of each variable into fewer groups, under all four tests. This collapsing reproduces one variable, employment. Collapsing full-time and part-time work into a single employed category and applying the Yates correction returns .625, which rounds to the reported value. The article describes no such collapsing and uses a continuity correction nowhere else. No collapsing of the marital status or DSM-IV diagnosis categories reproduces the reported p values under any of the four tests.

The irreproducible categorical p values do not affect the conclusions of the study. The reported and recalculated values are all above .05, so under either set the groups do not differ significantly on these characteristics. We count them because they are incorrect. A baseline table that does not reproduce from its own contingency table cells also limits how far a reader can verify the article’s other computations. The five p values that no test reproduces, from the reported cells or from any collapsing of them, are five instances of Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets (RIV 2.2). Employment is described but not counted.

3.3 Training intensity SDs that their ranges do not allow

The Results report the mean training intensity of the exercise group as “78% (SD = 24) of the 1RM (range 45–87%)”. Excluding the one subject who had less than 50% compliance, they report “80% (SD = 10) of the 1RM (range 70–87%)”. Both values are labeled SD in the text (the 1RM is the one-repetition maximum, the heaviest weight a subject can lift once, so intensity is the training load as a percentage of it). A set of nn values that lies within a range of width ww has an SD of at most w2n/(n−1)\tfrac{w}{2}\sqrt{n/(n-1)}, reached when half of the values sit at each end. For the 17 exercisers between 45% and 87%, the SD can be at most 22.2, even with each end of the range rounded outward by half a point. For the 16 exercisers between 70% and 87%, it can be at most 9.3. Neither reported SD is possible. Read as SEs, the implied SDs of 99 and 40 are larger still. No reading of these values is possible, so they are two instances of Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets (RIV 2.2). The Discussion repeats the first value (“mean 78, SD = 24”), which is described but not counted.

3.4 GRIM failure in a subgroup mean

The granularity-related inconsistency of means test (GRIM, Brown 2017) checks whether a reported mean can arise from integer scores, given the sample size. The BDI and HRSD are sums of integer item scores, so a group mean must be an integer divided by the group size. A mean reported to one decimal place can only fail the test when the group has fewer than 10 subjects. Of the 12 such means in Table 4, one fails. The baseline HRSD mean of 13.6 for the four control subjects with major depression would require an integer total between 54.2 and 54.6. No integer lies in that range (see Figure 9). The nearest achievable means are 13.5 and 13.75. The other 11 means are consistent.

Rounding, the scale, and the sample size do not explain the failure. The notes to Table 4 give the subgroup size as 4, which matches the DSM-IV diagnosis counts in Table 1 (2 mild and 2 moderate major depression). The HRSD is scored in whole points. The test already allows for rounding. The Results state that the improvement in HRSD scores in this subgroup was twice that of controls, a comparison that depends on the impossible mean. This is one instance of Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets (RIV 2.2).

The other values in the article that can be checked are consistent. The three correlations reported in the Results for the exercise group (r=r = −0.786, −0.589, and 0.500 with n=17n = 17) give p values of .0002, .013, and .04, against reported values of .0002, .013, and .04. The diagnostic subgroup sizes sum to the group sizes once the dysthymic subject in each group is included. The available evidence points to problems in how the statistics are labeled and reported. It gives no reason to doubt that the trial took place.

4 Conclusion

The audited article describes a small RCT whose tables misstate what they contain. Every table of continuous outcomes labels the values in parentheses as standard deviations, yet the Methods state that they are standard errors. The reported test statistics support the Methods (i.e., the values in parentheses are standard errors). For 25 variables across Tables 1 through 4, 6, and 7, the reported p value can not be reproduced with the values read as SDs and can be with the values read as SEs. Additionally, the reported age ranges and the Table 4 subgroup means are impossible under the labeled SDs. This warrants 86 instances of Data Miscalculation or Erroneous Reporting (RIV 2.4), one for each of the values involved.

The effect of this error on meta-analyses is the central finding of this report. The largest post-test SMD the article can support is −0.72 (−0.44 for the BDI). Of the 24 meta-analyses we located that report an effect size for this trial, 19 assign Singh (1997) a value beyond anything a correct extraction can produce. The median assigned value is −2.74. In two syntheses this 32-subject trial is the largest effect in the analysis by a factor of two or more. Four meta-analyses, including the Cochrane review, report exactly −1.75, the value that results from meta-analysts treating the standard errors of the post-intervention BDI scores as standard deviations. For nearly 30 years, the evidence base on exercise and depression has carried a moderate effect as one of its largest.

The columns labeled “Baseline p-value” in the outcome tables do not report baseline comparisons. In the one table where the SDs are unambiguous, 7 of the 8 reported p values can not be reproduced as a test of the baseline scores. Under the correct reading of the depression outcomes, the groups differed at baseline on the somatic subscale of the BDI, the outcome with the strongest reported interaction. Yet the Results state that no variable differed at baseline. The 13 reported values that no baseline test can return are 13 instances of Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets (RIV 2.2). Furthermore, three unpaired and five categorical p values in Tables 1 and 2 can not be reproduced under any reading of the reported summaries, which adds 8 instances of Statistical Inconsistencies and Impossible Data Sets (RIV 2.2). Two training intensity SDs in the Results are larger than their ranges allow under either reading, which adds two more. One subgroup mean in Table 4 is impossible given its sample size, which adds one.

Because the misreporting in Singh (1997) has contaminated the evidence syntheses in this field, we recommend retraction. Every table labels the standard errors as standard deviations. Of the 24 meta-analyses we located, 19 assign Singh (1997) an effect size that no correct extraction can produce. Four of these meta-analyses, including the Cochrane review, report the value that results from treating the standard errors as standard deviations. A reader can only use the published tables as evidence after first correcting them. For nearly 30 years, most meta-analysts have not. In addition, the outcome tables report p values under the label of a baseline comparison that no test of the baseline scores can return. Furthermore, 8 p values in Tables 1 and 2 can not be reproduced under any reading. One subgroup mean in Table 4 is impossible given its sample size. As published, the article’s results have no evidentiary value. This recommendation does not rest on any evidence that the trial was not conducted.

The retraction notice should give the reasons. These are that (a) the values in parentheses in Tables 1 through 4, 6, and 7 and in the abstract are standard errors, (b) the first p value column of Tables 3 through 7 does not report a comparison of the groups at baseline, (c) the groups differed at baseline on the somatic subscale of the BDI, (d) 8 p values in Tables 1 and 2 can not be reproduced from the reported summaries, (e) the subgroup mean in Table 4 is impossible given its sample size, and (f) the two training intensity SDs in the Results are impossible given their ranges.

Whatever the journal decides, meta-analysts and guideline developers who have included this trial should check the values they extracted from it. A meta-analysis that recorded the values in parentheses as standard deviations has treated a moderate effect as a very large one. Such a meta-analysis should re-extract the study with those values multiplied by the square root of the group size, or exclude the study.
